The questions vets actually ask

Shining UV into an eye sounds alarming. Here's the honest answer.

Dose

It's a low dose, not a sterilising lamp

The germicidal fixtures you're picturing run for minutes. This is a metered five-second pulse at 265 nm — the wavelength where microbial DNA absorbs most strongly and the dose needed is smallest.

Tissue

UVC is absorbed at the surface

265 nm doesn't travel like visible light. It's absorbed in the outermost corneal tissue, which is exactly where infectious keratitis lives — and why safety work has focused on corneal DNA and the limbus. Both are published above.

Handling

Nothing touches the eye

Non-contact means no applicator on an already-painful cornea, no cross-contamination between patients, and far less restraint. Nurses report animals barely react.

Resistance

There is no resistance pathway

Antibiotic resistance is a biochemical adaptation. UVC damages DNA directly. A population can't evolve around physics the way it evolves around a molecule.

Adjunct

It doesn't replace your judgement

Culture and cytology still matter. UVC gives you an immediate broad-spectrum action while you wait for results — most useful in the first 48 hours, when you're guessing.

Trial

Try it in your own clinic first

Demo units go out with the full paper set. Use it on your own caseload for two weeks. If your team doesn't reach for it, send it back.

In the consult room

Three steps, under a minute.

Position

Hand-held or mounted on your slit lamp. Set the spacer at the working distance and line up on the lesion. No drops, no gel, no contact.

Treat

One press. Five seconds. The device meters the dose itself, so the treatment your nurse delivers is the treatment your ophthalmologist would.

Recheck

Continue your normal protocol and reassess. Most practices recheck at 24 hours and repeat only if the lesion warrants it.
